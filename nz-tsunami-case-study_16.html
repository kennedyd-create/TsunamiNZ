Situation report

One morning, three big earthquakes

At 2.27am on Friday 5 March 2021, a magnitude 7.3 earthquake struck about 100 km off East Cape. It woke most of the North Island and set a national record for felt reports. Four hours later a M7.4 struck near the Kermadec Islands, roughly 1,000 km north-east — and less than two hours after that, a M8.1 in almost the same place. All three generated tsunami. Thousands of New Zealanders evacuated, some more than once, and the whole national warning system was tested in real time for the first time in years.

Why does this keep happening to us?

Aotearoa New Zealand sits directly on the join between two of the planet's largest tectonic plates. That join is the reason we have mountains, geothermal fields, and about 15,000 earthquakes a year. It is also the reason a wave can be at our coast in under an hour. In this section you'll work out exactly what kind of boundary we're sitting on, what a megathrust earthquake is, and why only some earthquakes make tsunami.

The wave that isn't a wave

A tsunami is not a big surf wave. A surf wave is made by wind pushing on the surface of the sea, and it only moves the top few metres of water. A tsunami is made when something shoves the entire water column — from the seafloor all the way to the surface — upwards or sideways all at once. That is why the four things that cause them are all sudden, violent movements of the seabed or the coast: undersea earthquakes, underwater or coastal landslides, volcanic eruptions, and, very rarely, a large meteorite strike.

Most tsunami come from earthquakes, but not from just any earthquake. Three things have to line up. The quake has to be large, usually magnitude 7.5 or above. It has to be shallow, so the movement reaches the seafloor rather than being absorbed by tens of kilometres of rock. And critically, it has to move the seafloor vertically. A strike-slip fault, where two blocks grind past each other sideways, usually displaces very little water. A subduction zone megathrust, where one plate is dragged down and the plate above it suddenly springs upward, lifts a slab of ocean the size of a small country. That is where the giants come from: Sumatra in 2004, Japan in 2011, and — one day — the Hikurangi Margin.

In the open ocean a tsunami is almost invisible. Its amplitude may be under a metre and its wavelength hundreds of kilometres, so a ship in deep water can pass straight over one without noticing. But it is carrying an enormous amount of energy, and it is travelling at up to 800 kilometres per hour. As it reaches the continental shelf the water gets shallower, the front of the wave slows down, the back of it catches up, and all of that energy compresses into a much shorter, much taller wall of water. This is called shoaling.

What arrives at the shore is not one wave either. It is a wave train — a series of surges that can keep coming for twelve hours or more, and the first one is very often not the biggest. Sometimes the sea drains away from the beach first, exposing the seafloor, which is one of nature's clearest warnings and one that has killed people who walked out to look. In New Zealand there have been about ten recorded tsunami with wave heights over four metres at the coastline since 1840. Most of them were generated by seafloor earthquakes not far off our own coast.

Now make it about home

Waiheke is an island of roughly 9,000 permanent residents that can swell to many times that in summer. Nearly everything that matters here — the ferry terminals, the shops, the main roads, several schools and a lot of houses — is at or near sea level. There are no tsunami sirens. If the Hikurangi Margin ruptures, the official warning may not reach you before the water does. So this section has one job: work out what you would actually do, and then design something better for everyone else.

Task 1

Red, orange, yellow — what do they actually mean?

0 / 5
▸ Do this

Read the three zone cards, then the note about Auckland underneath. Tick off what you have learned, then answer the three questions — one of them is about which source you should trust.

Red — shore & marine

The beach, the water and the land right beside it. Affected even by a small tsunami. This is the zone most often evacuated, and you should stay out of it for any tsunami alert.

Orange — large tsunami

Low-lying land further back that would flood in a large tsunami. Evacuated when an official warning says so — or immediately, if you feel a long or strong earthquake.

Yellow — maximum

The furthest inland the water could ever reach, in a rare and very large event. In most of Aotearoa you must clear all zones, yellow included, if the shaking is long or strong.

🎨 A note on colour in this resource. Red, orange and yellow are used for one thing only — evacuation zones. Everywhere else you will see navy, gold, blue and green, so a colour never means two different things.
🗺️ Auckland is different — and this is a real research trap. In February 2024, Auckland Emergency Management moved to a simpler two-colour system after new wave modelling: a red shore-and-marine threat zone and a yellow land threat zone. Older worksheets, and plenty of websites, still describe three zones for Auckland. When two sources disagree, the more recent official one wins — and you should say in your work which one you used and why.
